01The story in one picture

Setup: You're a college student (engineering, CS, data science…) with a side skill: you cook chicken really well. You invite 10 friends, they love it and give great feedback. That makes you think: "Can I start a small business?" Food-delivery platforms such as Zomato and Swiggy make that easy, so you open an account and list your "special chicken". A delivery person picks orders up and takes them to customers.

Then orders keep growing, and each jump in orders brings a new problem:

02Stage by stage

Stage 1: Launch (1–2 orders/day) 06:20

You list "special chicken" on Zomato. A delivery person picks up each order. With one or two orders a day you handle everything yourself, and nothing is a problem yet.

Stage 2: Work harder (4–5 orders/day) 07:24

People like the food, and after a few weeks you get 4–5 orders a day. You go to the market, buy chicken, potato and spicy masala, combine them into your recipe and cook more. It's still manageable if you put in extra effort.

Stage 3: Suppliers + inventory (10–15 orders/day) 09:32

Problem

After a month you get 10–15 orders a day. Going to the market 10–15 times a day while also studying is impossible. You need more business with less effort.

Fix

Contract a chicken shop, a potato shop and a masala shop. On the first day of each month they deliver enough ingredients for the month to an inventory room in your home. The time you used to spend on market trips now goes into cooking.

Maps to → Data management

Ingredients are the data. Different shops are different data sources. The inventory room is a central store (a data warehouse). Instead of fetching raw material by hand each time, it arrives reliably in one place.

Stage 4: Hire a junior + recipe book (≈ 30–40 orders/day) 12:47

Problem

Demand grows again, so you hire a junior, Alex, and split orders (about 20 each). Soon bad reviews start coming in, all from Alex's orders. He doesn't know your special recipe and cooks his own way, so customers don't get the taste they expect.

Fix

Write a Recipe Book (RB), like a restaurant's menu guide with exact instructions. Both of you cook from the same book, so every dish tastes the same. Good reviews return, and the business grows steadily.

Maps to → Version control

Several people working on the same product need one shared source of truth. In software that is Git/GitHub: everyone works from the same versioned code, so nobody's output drifts from the agreed "recipe".

Stage 5: Buy a cooking machine (50–80 orders/day) 17:01

Problem

Orders climb to 50–60, then 70–80 a day. You could keep hiring, but a home kitchen is small, and renting a bigger kitchen raises costs, which you want to avoid.

Fix

Buy an automatic cooking machine. It reads the recipe book, takes ingredients from inventory, cooks and outputs about 40 portions per batch. It can run many batches a day. Only one junior is needed to load ingredients, hand orders to delivery, and monitor the machine.

Maps to → Automation / pipeline

The machine repeats the same ordered steps every time: take ingredients → follow recipe → cook → deliver. The instructor's definition: "a pipeline means steps of a task" that run in a fixed order, repeatedly, without manual work.

Stage 6: Saturation → experiments → continuous updates 20:15

Problem

You monitor your growth graph and see it flatten (a saturation point). Customers are bored: after ordering the same dish 5–7 times, people want a different taste.

Fix

Set up a small research room with a sample of the inventory. Run experiments (below), pick the best recipe and launch it as a new dish.

ExperimentChange triedResult
Ex 1More potatoTested
Ex 2Sweet masala instead of spicyTested
Ex 3More potato + sweet-spicy masala + a little more saltBest taste → selected
Ex 4Extra spicyTested

Experiment 3 becomes a new menu item, "Sweet Chicken", next to the original "Chicken Special". Selecting it triggers two updates automatically:

  1. The Zomato menu is updated, so customers see and try the new dish.
  2. The recipe book is updated with Ex 3's ingredients, so the machine starts cooking the new dish.

Customers try it, reviews are good, and the growth curve rises again. A person keeps watching reviews. If they turn bad, the experiment cycle starts again. The business, which began at 1–2 orders, now handles 100–200 orders a day almost entirely automatically.

05The 7 components of MLOps 29:42

After the story, the instructor lists the core components of MLOps:

1

Data management

Getting data from many sources into one reliable place.

2

Version control

Tracking versions of code (and data) so people can collaborate and roll back.

3

Automation / pipeline

Ordered, repeatable steps that run without manual work.

4

Experiment tracking

Trying many variants, logging them all, choosing the best.

5

CI/CD

Continuous integration and continuous delivery/deployment: changes go live automatically.

6

Monitoring

Watching the live system and its quality.

7

Retraining

Re-running the pipeline when quality drops.

+ extras

Provisioning, security

Mentioned but set aside for now: infrastructure provisioning and security layers.

The takeaway

The business went from 1–2 orders to 100–200 orders a day because each bottleneck was fixed with structure and automation, not because the cook worked harder. That is the goal of MLOps for an ML application.

07Mapping to a real ML project 27:34

The instructor uses the US Visa approval prediction project (a classification problem) as the ML example.

Initial phase = Stage 1 of the kitchen

Like the cook at 1–2 orders, this is fine at first. When a massive audience arrives, it fails, because there is no infrastructure or architecture behind it.

With MLOps = the fully automated kitchen 34:00

Step by step, as the instructor describes it:

  1. Data management: data comes from APIs, web scraping, CSV files and databases. Store all of it in one main database / data warehouse.
  2. Pipeline (automation): take data from the warehouse and run the same steps every time: EDA → feature engineering → model building.
  3. Experimentation: one model gives one accuracy. For better performance, try Random Forest, Decision Tree, KNN with different parameters and pick the one with the best accuracy.
  4. Update the pipeline with the chosen model.
  5. CI/CD → deployment/serving: based on the experiment, the model in production updates automatically.
  6. Version control: with Git/GitHub, a new architecture becomes a new version instead of replacing the old one.
  7. Monitoring: is the app working well in production?
  8. Retraining: if not, check the data and retrain.
Beyond the video · production view

In a real deployment, "Best model → Deploy" usually goes through a model registry (for example, MLflow Model Registry). The pipeline registers the model, CI/CD builds a Docker image that loads the registered version, and a web API (FastAPI/Flask) serves predictions. You'll build this path in the end-to-end project section of the course (4:52:33).

09Interview questions

Explain MLOps to a non-technical person.

Use the kitchen story: a home cook scales to hundreds of orders by (1) getting ingredients delivered to one storeroom (data management), (2) writing a shared recipe book (version control), (3) buying a machine that follows the recipe (pipeline automation), (4) testing new recipes in a research room (experiment tracking), (5) pushing the winning recipe to the menu and machine automatically (CI/CD), (6) watching reviews (monitoring), and (7) re-experimenting when reviews drop (retraining).

What are the core components of an MLOps system?

Data management, version control, automation/pipelines, experiment tracking, CI/CD, monitoring and retraining. Extra concerns include infrastructure provisioning and security.

Why isn't a single trained model enough?

One model gives one accuracy on one dataset. You improve it by experimenting (different algorithms, hyperparameters) and tracking every run. Over time, user behaviour and data change (the "customers get bored" moment), so a model that was best once will plateau or degrade. You need monitoring and retraining to stay good.

What is a pipeline in ML?

An ordered sequence of steps (for example ingestion → validation → transformation → training → evaluation) that runs the same way every time without manual work. It makes results reproducible and lets you re-run everything with one command when data changes.

What does "CI/CD" mean for ML?

Continuous Integration: every change (code, config, chosen model) is automatically integrated and tested. Continuous Delivery/Deployment: the tested result is automatically released to production. In the story, choosing Ex 3 automatically updated both the menu and the machine.

10Recap

  • Each growth stage exposed a bottleneck; each fix is an MLOps component.
  • Inventory = data management · Recipe book = version control · Machine = pipeline · Research room = experiment tracking · Auto-update of menu + machine = CI/CD · Review watcher = monitoring · Re-experiment = retraining.
  • An ML project without these works for 10–15 users and breaks at scale.
  • MLOps components each have tools: DVC, Git/GitHub, SageMaker, MLflow, Jenkins/CircleCI/GitHub Actions, Grafana, Airflow.
